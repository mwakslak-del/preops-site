Do I Need a Cardiac Evaluation Before Surgery?

If your surgeon has asked for a cardiac evaluation, it does not mean something is wrong with your heart. It means your surgical team wants one question answered carefully before the day of surgery, while there is still time to act on the answer.

By Menachem Wakslak, MD, board-certified cardiologist · Reviewed November 2026

Why surgeons ask

An operation asks more of the heart than an ordinary day does. Anesthesia, shifts in blood pressure and fluids, and the body's response to healing all add to its workload for a few days. Most hearts handle this without difficulty. The purpose of a cardiac evaluation is to find the people for whom a little preparation makes surgery safer, and to say plainly when no preparation is needed.

A good evaluation answers three questions:

  • Is there a heart condition that should be treated first? This is uncommon, and it is far better to learn it now than in the operating room.
  • What is the level of risk for this patient and this operation? The answer should be an honest estimate, not a guess.
  • What is the plan? Which medicines to continue, which to pause and when, and what the surgical team should know about a pacemaker, a defibrillator, or a blood thinner.

Who usually needs one

Your surgeon or primary care doctor is likely to ask for a cardiac evaluation if any of these apply to you:

  • Known heart disease: a past heart attack, stents or bypass surgery, heart failure, a valve problem, or a rhythm problem such as atrial fibrillation
  • A pacemaker or an implanted defibrillator
  • A blood thinner or antiplatelet medicine that needs a plan around surgery
  • Symptoms such as chest discomfort, shortness of breath with activity, fainting, or palpitations
  • A heart murmur, or an abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG)
  • A stroke or mini-stroke in the recent past
  • A larger operation, when it is hard to tell how well your heart tolerates exertion

Who usually does not

If you have no heart disease, no symptoms, and no major risk factors, and you are having a low-risk procedure such as cataract surgery, a skin procedure, or a routine endoscopy, current guidelines say that no special cardiac evaluation is needed. A thoughtful evaluation service should be willing to tell you so. More testing is not the same as more safety, and a surgery date should not be lost to a workup that was never necessary.

Evaluation, not “clearance”

You may hear this visit called a cardiac clearance. We prefer the word evaluation, and the difference is more than vocabulary. No one can promise that an operation carries no risk, so no one can truly “clear” a patient. What we can do is more useful: estimate the risk honestly, lower it where that is possible, and give your surgeon and anesthesiologist a written plan they can act on.

The evaluation ends in one of three ways:

  • Proceed. Nothing further is needed. This is the most common result.
  • Proceed, with specific steps. For example, a schedule for pausing a blood thinner, or instructions for a pacemaker.
  • Defer, for a stated reason. Something should be treated first. When this happens, we call your surgeon's office the same day, so that everyone can plan together.

Two pieces of practical advice

Go early. If a test turns out to be useful, there is time to do it without moving your surgery date. The evaluation that causes delays is usually the one that was scheduled late.

Do not stop any medicine on your own. Some heart medicines protect you during surgery and should be taken right up to the morning of the operation. Others need to be paused on a precise schedule. The evaluation is where that plan gets written down, for you and for your surgical team.

This guide offers general information and is not personal medical advice. Your own doctors know your situation and should guide your decisions. Our approach follows the 2024 AHA/ACC guideline for perioperative cardiovascular management.
