Getting Ready for Surgery: What the Evidence Says You Can Do Yourself

The weeks before an operation are not just waiting time. There is real evidence that what you do in them can affect how you recover. The evidence is also uneven, and some popular advice runs ahead of it. This guide separates what is well supported from what is merely plausible.

By Menachem Wakslak, MD, board-certified cardiologist · Reviewed November 2026

What “prehabilitation” means

Rehabilitation helps you recover after an operation. Prehabilitation, or prehab, is the same idea applied beforehand: building strength, stamina, and nutritional reserve in the weeks before surgery so that you start from a better place. It usually combines exercise and nutrition, sometimes with help for anxiety or for stopping smoking.

What the research shows overall

The largest review to date, published in 2025, combined 186 randomized trials involving more than 15,000 patients. Exercise before surgery was associated with roughly half the risk of complications, and nutritional support with a reduction of about a third. Hospital stays were about a day shorter.1 Those are encouraging numbers, but the authors rated the certainty of the evidence as low to very low. Many of the trials were small, were run at single hospitals, and used closely supervised programs. The fair summary is that prehab probably helps, and that we are less sure by how much.

Can you do it on your own?

This is the question that matters to most people. Much of the favorable evidence comes from supervised programs, in which patients trained with a therapist several times a week. One well-run trial of this kind, in people having colorectal cancer surgery, found meaningfully fewer serious complications after four weeks of supervised exercise, nutrition, and psychological support.2

Home programs have been harder to prove. The largest test so far, a Canadian trial of 847 older adults with frailty, gave patients a home exercise and nutrition plan with a weekly coaching call. Across the whole group, it did not reduce complications or disability after surgery. But fewer than half of the participants completed most of their exercises. Those who did had less disability a month after surgery than patients given usual care.3

That comparison is not proof, because people who manage to stick to a program may differ in other ways. Still, the lesson is a sensible one: a home plan is safe and inexpensive, and it can only help if it is actually done. The main obstacles in the trial were ordinary ones: competing priorities and motivation.

A reasonable plan to follow at home

The home programs that have been studied look much alike, and a plan modeled on them is a sound place to start:

  • Walk every day. Aim for a pace that makes you breathe harder but still lets you talk.
  • Three times a week, do a longer session: about 20 minutes of moderate aerobic activity such as brisk walking or cycling, simple strength exercises such as rising from a chair, wall push-ups, or light weights, and some stretching.
  • Start as early as you can. Most studied programs ran for about four weeks. Even two or three weeks is worth doing.
  • Make it routine. A set time of day, a walking partner, or a simple log improves the odds that it happens.

If you have heart or lung disease, or you have not been active, ask your doctor what is safe before you begin. Stop and call your doctor if exercise brings on chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, lightheadedness, or palpitations.

One observational study adds a useful benchmark. Among patients whose activity trackers recorded their steps before surgery, those averaging fewer than about 7,500 steps a day had roughly twice the odds of a complication.4 That is an association, not proof that walking more prevents complications. More active people tend to be healthier to begin with.

If you smoke, this is the most valuable change

The evidence here is the strongest of any step on this list. In randomized trials, smokers who received intensive help to quit, meaning weekly counseling and nicotine replacement beginning four to eight weeks before surgery, had fewer than half as many complications overall and about a third as many wound complications. Brief advice alone did not show the same benefit.5 Ask your doctor for a real quit plan with medication and support, and start as far ahead as you can. Stopping later is still better than not stopping.

Alcohol

For people who drink heavily, trials of intensive programs to stop drinking for four to eight weeks before surgery found about a third fewer complications.6 The trials were small, so the size of the benefit is uncertain. One caution is essential: if you drink heavily every day, do not stop abruptly on your own. Sudden withdrawal can be dangerous. Tell your doctor and your surgical team honestly how much you drink, so that it can be managed safely before and after the operation.

Learn your breathing exercises before the operation

A trial of 441 patients having major upper abdominal surgery tested something modest: a single 30-minute session, before surgery, teaching patients why lung complications happen and how to prevent them with deep-breathing exercises and early walking afterward. Pneumonia and related complications were cut by about half. For every seven patients taught, one was spared a lung complication.7 If you are having abdominal or chest surgery, ask to be shown these exercises ahead of time, and begin them as soon as you are awake.

Eat well, and follow your surgeon's advice on weight

Your body needs protein and energy to heal, so regular meals with adequate protein are a reasonable aim for most people in the weeks before surgery. Weight is a separate question, and the right answer depends on the operation. Before some procedures, such as hernia repair or joint replacement, your surgeon may ask you to lose weight first. If so, follow that plan, and ask how to do it while still getting enough protein. What is best avoided is a crash diet started on your own shortly before surgery.

Unplanned weight loss is different. Nutrition guidelines advise that people who are undernourished, or who have lost weight without trying, should receive nutritional support before major surgery, sometimes for ten to fourteen days, even if that means a short delay.8 If you have lost weight unintentionally, have a poor appetite, or are eating much less than usual, tell your surgical team. Follow the fasting instructions they give you for the day of surgery.

What belongs with your doctors

Some preparation cannot be self-directed. Decisions about blood thinners, diabetes medicines, blood pressure medicines, and anemia should be made with your doctors. Tell your surgical team about every supplement and over-the-counter product you take, since some affect bleeding or interact with anesthesia. Do not stop or start a medicine on your own.

In short

  • Stopping smoking, with real support, has the best evidence. Start four to eight weeks ahead if you can.
  • Regular exercise before surgery probably helps, and it helps only if you do it. Walk daily and add three longer sessions a week.
  • Learn breathing exercises before abdominal or chest surgery.
  • Eat regular meals with adequate protein. Follow your surgeon's advice on weight, and report any unintended weight loss.
  • Be honest about alcohol, and do not stop heavy drinking abruptly without medical help.
  • Leave medication decisions to your doctors.
