Stress Test Before Surgery: When It Helps and When It Does Not

Many people assume that a heart evaluation before surgery means a stress test. In fact, most patients do not need one. A stress test is valuable when its result would change what happens next. When it would not, it adds delay and worry without adding safety.

By Menachem Wakslak, MD, board-certified cardiologist · Reviewed November 2026

The question that decides it

Before ordering any test, we ask one question: would the result change the plan? That might mean a change in whether to operate, when, what kind of operation or anesthesia is chosen, or how you are monitored afterward. If every possible result would lead to the same plan, the test is not worth doing. This principle sits at the center of the national guidelines that cardiologists, surgeons, and anesthesiologists share, and it serves patients well.

When a stress test is usually unnecessary

  • The procedure is low risk. Cataract surgery, skin procedures, routine endoscopy, and most minor outpatient operations place little strain on the heart. Guidelines advise against stress testing before them.
  • You are active without symptoms. If you can climb a flight of stairs or walk up a hill, do heavy housework, or keep up a brisk walk without chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness, your heart has in effect already passed a stress test. Daily life has been testing it for you.
  • Your heart condition is stable. Having coronary disease, or a stent placed some time ago, does not by itself call for a new test if you feel well and remain active.

When it is worth considering

A stress test earns its place when several things are true together: the operation is a larger one, your overall risk is elevated, and no one can tell how your heart tolerates exertion. That last point is more common than you might think. Arthritis in a knee or hip, a back problem, or lung disease can limit activity so much that the heart is never really challenged. In that situation, with a major operation ahead, we lean toward testing, because the answer is otherwise unknown and it may matter.

New or changing symptoms are a separate matter. Chest pressure, breathlessness that has worsened, or fainting deserve evaluation whether or not surgery is planned.

What the test involves

If you are able to walk on a treadmill, that is the preferred approach, often combined with ultrasound images of the heart taken before and immediately after exercise. This is called a stress echocardiogram, and we perform it in our office. It answers two questions in one visit: how much exertion your heart can manage, and whether any part of the heart muscle is short of blood flow when it works hard.

If you cannot exercise, a medication can stand in for the treadmill while images of the heart are taken. That type of test is done at an outside imaging center. We arrange it and let your surgeon's office know when to expect the result.

Why not test everyone, to be safe?

It is a reasonable question, and the answer surprises many people. Testing everyone does not make surgery safer, for two reasons.

First, stress tests are much better at giving reassurance than at predicting trouble. A normal result is strong evidence that the heart will tolerate surgery. An abnormal result is far less certain. Most patients with an abnormal preoperative stress test go on to have no heart complication at all. Testing people who are at low risk therefore produces mostly false alarms, and each false alarm can lead to further tests, postponed operations, and sometimes invasive procedures that carry risks of their own.

Second, careful studies have shown that for most people with stable coronary disease, opening a narrowed artery with a stent or bypass before an operation does not lower the risk of that operation. The exceptions are specific and uncommon, such as an unstable heart condition or a severe narrowing of the main artery supplying the left side of the heart. So even a truly abnormal result often does not change what is done, which is why the question of whether the result would change the plan has to be asked first.

If a test is abnormal

An abnormal result opens a conversation. It does not automatically cancel your surgery. Depending on what is found, the right course may be to proceed with adjusted medicines and closer monitoring, to choose a less demanding operation or anesthetic, or, less often, to treat the heart first. That decision is made together by you, your surgeon, and us, with the reasons explained to you.

A stress test is not an echocardiogram

The two are often confused. An echocardiogram is a resting ultrasound that shows the heart's structure, its pumping strength, and its valves. It is commonly useful before surgery when there is a murmur, shortness of breath, or known heart disease without a recent study. A stress test asks a different question: what happens when the heart is made to work. Needing one does not mean you need the other.

The aim throughout is the same: to do what will make your surgery safer, and to leave out what will not.

This guide offers general information and is not personal medical advice. Your own doctors know your situation and should guide your decisions. Our approach follows the 2024 AHA/ACC guideline for perioperative cardiovascular management.
