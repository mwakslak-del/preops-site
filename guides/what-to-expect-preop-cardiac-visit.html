What Happens at Your Preoperative Cardiac Visit

Most of the worry before a cardiac evaluation comes from not knowing what the visit involves. It is a focused visit with one purpose: to help you get to and through your surgery safely. Here is what to expect, from the first phone call to the letter your surgeon receives.

By Menachem Wakslak, MD, board-certified cardiologist · Reviewed November 2026

Before the visit

The visit starts with a referral. Your primary care doctor or your surgeon will need to send one to us. Once we have it, we call you to schedule. We set the visit as early as your surgery date allows, so that if any testing proves useful there is time to do it.

Before you come in, we try to obtain the records of any heart testing you have had in the past, such as an ECG, an echocardiogram, a stress test, or a hospital stay. Earlier results give us something to compare against, and they often spare you from repeating a test. It helps if you can tell us where and roughly when that testing was done.

Please bring:

  • All of your medication bottles, or an accurate list with doses
  • Your insurance card and a photo ID
  • The card for your pacemaker or defibrillator, if you have one
  • Any heart records you already have at home, in case we were not able to get them

Keep taking your medicines as usual before the visit. Please do not stop anything in anticipation of surgery until we have gone over it together.

In the room

The visit begins with a conversation. We ask about your heart history, your symptoms, and the operation you are having. We also ask about your ordinary activity: whether you can climb a flight of stairs, carry groceries, or work in the yard. These questions may seem simple, but they are among the most informative parts of the evaluation. How your heart handles daily exertion tells us a great deal about how it will handle surgery.

We then examine you: blood pressure, heart rate and rhythm, and a careful listen to your heart and lungs. For most patients we record an electrocardiogram (ECG) in the office. It takes a few minutes and is painless. If you have an earlier ECG, we compare the two, because a finding that has been stable for years means something quite different from one that is new.

Tests, only when they help

Many patients need nothing beyond the conversation, the examination, and the ECG. When a test would change the plan, we arrange it, and most can be done in our office:

  • Echocardiogram. An ultrasound of the heart that shows how well it pumps and how the valves are working. It is useful for a murmur, for shortness of breath, or when there is known heart disease and no recent study.
  • Treadmill test or stress echocardiogram. These show how the heart responds to exertion. They are considered before a larger operation when it is unclear how much activity your heart can tolerate.
  • Heart rhythm monitor. A small wearable recorder, used when a rhythm problem is suspected.
  • Blood tests. Sometimes helpful in estimating risk. These are drawn at a nearby laboratory.

Occasionally a test that we do not perform in the office is the right one, such as a stress test that uses medication in place of exercise. In that case we schedule it for you and tell your surgeon's office when to expect the result.

What your surgeon receives

After the visit we send your surgeon a written evaluation, sometimes called a cardiac clearance letter, together with the consult note and the reports of any tests. It states the estimated risk, any steps to take before or during surgery, and a clear recommendation: proceed, proceed with specific steps, or defer for a stated reason. Copies go to your primary care doctor and to your own cardiologist if you have one.

If we find anything that could delay your operation, we call your surgeon's office before the letter goes out. No one should learn of a problem on the morning of surgery.

What you take home

You leave with written instructions for your heart medicines: which to keep taking, which to pause, the date of the last dose, and when to restart. This matters most for blood thinners, for certain diabetes and weight-loss medicines, and for some blood pressure medicines. Your surgical team will confirm the instructions for the morning of surgery when they call you beforehand.

Afterward

Ours is a preoperative service. If the evaluation uncovers something that deserves ongoing attention, we make sure your primary care doctor and your own cardiologist know about it, so that it does not get lost once the surgery is behind you. If you have questions at any point, you are welcome to call or text us at 818-724-8200.

This guide offers general information and is not personal medical advice. Your own doctors know your situation and should guide your decisions. Our approach follows the 2024 AHA/ACC guideline for perioperative cardiovascular management.
